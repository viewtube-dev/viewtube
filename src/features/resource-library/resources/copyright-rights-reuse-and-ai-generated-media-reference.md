# Copyright, Rights, Reuse and AI-Generated Media Reference

A creator's video may contain footage, music, photographs, graphics, fonts, voices, performances, logos, and AI-generated or AI-modified material. Each item can have different rights, license terms, restrictions, and disclosure considerations. Finding a file online—or generating it with a tool—does not automatically mean you may use it in any way you choose.

> **Creator principle:** Record where each asset came from, who controls it, what permission or license applies, and what limits remain. Credit is not a substitute for permission.

This guide is operational education, not legal advice. Copyright, publicity rights, privacy, contracts, platform policies, and exceptions vary by jurisdiction and circumstance. For a consequential use or dispute, consult a qualified legal professional.

## The Creator Mental Model

For every third-party or generated asset, ask four questions:

1. **Source:** Where did this asset come from, and can I verify that source?
2. **Rights:** Who owns or controls the relevant rights?
3. **Permission:** What uses are permitted, and under which conditions?
4. **Proof:** What record shows that I am allowed to use it in this project?

The answers may differ for a music track, a stock photo, a public-domain image, a collaborator's voice, or an AI-generated scene based on a real person. A spreadsheet or project asset record can help preserve the evidence, but it does not itself create permission.

## Copyright, License and Attribution Are Different

**Copyright** can give rights holders control over copying, distribution, adaptation, public performance or other uses, depending on the work and applicable law.

**A license** grants permission for specified uses under specified conditions. Read the actual terms. A license may restrict commercial use, modification, redistribution, territory, duration, or use in particular contexts.

**Attribution** identifies a source or creator. Some licenses require it, but credit alone does not grant rights.

**Public domain** generally means a work is not protected by copyright or its copyright has expired or been waived under applicable rules. The status can be difficult to establish and may differ by country. A work's online availability does not make it public domain.

**Fair use and similar exceptions** are legal doctrines whose application depends on facts and jurisdiction. There is no universal number of seconds, words, or percentage that automatically makes a use lawful.

## Build an Asset Rights Record

For each meaningful asset, record:

| Field | What to capture |
|---|---|
| Asset name | A clear label and project reference |
| Source | Original page, provider, creator, or tool |
| Rights holder | Who owns or controls the relevant rights, if known |
| License or permission | Exact license name, terms, contract or permission record |
| Allowed use | Monetization, editing, redistribution, territory, duration, platform or other conditions |
| Attribution | Required credit wording and placement |
| Restrictions | Prohibited contexts, alterations, sublicensing or other limits |
| Evidence | Saved receipt, license text, permission email, contract or source record |
| Review date | When the terms were checked |
| Status | Verified, conditional, unclear, or not approved |

Keep the record with the project and link it to the actual asset. If the license page changes, retain a dated copy of the applicable terms where lawful and appropriate.

## Music, Footage, Images and Fonts

### Music and sound

A recording and the underlying musical composition may involve different rights. A track that is licensed for personal use may not be licensed for monetized video. Check the license and platform-specific conditions, including attribution and any restrictions on edits or distribution.

### Stock footage and photographs

Verify the provider and license, not just the download button. Some assets have model or property release requirements, limits on sensitive uses, or restrictions on redistribution. Avoid implying that a person endorses a product or statement unless you have the necessary rights and factual basis.

### Fonts, graphics and templates

A font or template may allow use in a finished video but restrict redistribution of the underlying file. Read the license for the intended use and keep the original source. “Free” may refer to price rather than unrestricted permission.

### Collaborators and guests

Written agreements can clarify permission to use footage, voice, likeness, performance, edits, and promotional excerpts. Keep expectations clear before production, especially for paid collaborations or sensitive subjects.

## Fair Use: Avoid Shortcut Myths

Fair use analysis in the United States can consider factors such as purpose and character, nature of the original work, amount and substantiality used, and effect on the potential market. Other jurisdictions may apply different exceptions and tests.

No single factor guarantees the result. Commentary, criticism, education, or adding your own voice may be relevant, but the label alone does not make a use fair. Using only a short excerpt is not automatically safe if it captures the heart of the work. Conversely, a use involving more material is not automatically unlawful in every circumstance.

Ask what purpose the excerpt serves, whether the amount is justified, whether the use adds meaningful new context, and what legal rules apply. Do not rely on a checklist as a legal ruling.

## Reusing Your Own Material Is Different From Reusing Someone Else's

Repurposing your own material can save effort, but check whether a collaborator, employer, music provider, stock service, or contract controls some relevant rights. Reuse rights for an original file may differ from the rights in music, footage, or a performance included in it.

Copyright permission and platform monetization eligibility are also different questions. Content may be permitted to remain on the platform but still face monetization limitations under current policies; conversely, monetization status does not itself establish that all rights are cleared. Review each applicable rule independently.

## AI-Generated and Altered Media

AI tools can generate images, video, music, voices, and other material. Before using output, check the tool's current terms, the rights in input material, the intended use, and any relevant platform disclosure requirements.

Consider these questions:

- Did the input include copyrighted work, a real person's likeness, a private recording, or confidential information?
- Does the output imitate a recognizable person, voice, or protected style in a way that raises rights or deception concerns?
- Does the tool's license allow the intended commercial or public use?
- Could a viewer reasonably mistake realistic synthetic content for an actual person, event, statement, or recording?
- Does the platform currently require disclosure for this kind of realistic altered or synthetic media?
- Have you preserved the prompt, source assets, tool/version, and applicable terms where useful?

AI output is not automatically free of third-party rights or risk. Tool terms can change; keep a record of the terms that applied when you created the asset.

## Copyright Claims and Platform Processes

A platform claim, takedown notice, strike, or monetization restriction can have different meanings and consequences. Read the exact notice and current official instructions. Do not assume every claim is invalid, or that every claim proves infringement in the same way.

Before disputing a claim, understand the content at issue, your rights or legal basis, the potential consequences, and available options. Do not submit a counter-notification or legal statement casually. Seek qualified legal advice if the consequences are significant.

## Worked Example: “I Gave Credit, So It's Fine”

A creator finds a popular song, adds the artist's name in the description, and uses it as background music. The creator has provided attribution, but has not established permission. The correct next step is to check whether the track is licensed for the intended use, replace it with appropriately licensed audio, or obtain permission. Credit is important when required, but it is not the permission itself.

## Creator Action Checklist

- [ ] Identify every third-party and generated asset in the final video.
- [ ] Verify the source and rights holder where possible.
- [ ] Read the license or obtain explicit permission.
- [ ] Record allowed use, restrictions and required attribution.
- [ ] Keep evidence connected to the project and asset.
- [ ] Review realistic synthetic-media disclosure rules.
- [ ] Resolve uncertainty before publication when the risk is consequential.
- [ ] Recheck current platform instructions before disputing a claim.

## Glossary

**License** — Permission to use a work under stated terms.  
**Attribution** — Credit identifying a source or creator.  
**Fair use** — A fact-specific U.S. copyright doctrine; not a universal shortcut or fixed percentage rule.  
**Rights holder** — A person or organization controlling relevant rights.  
**Synthetic media** — Media generated or materially altered using computational tools, including AI.  
**Counter-notification** — A formal response to certain copyright takedown processes, with legal implications.
